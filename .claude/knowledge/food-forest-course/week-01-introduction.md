## Key Concepts

### The Seven Layers of a Food Forest
1. **Canopy / Tall Tree Layer** — Large fruit and nut trees (e.g., walnut, chestnut, large apple)
2. **Understory / Small Tree Layer** — Smaller trees growing in dappled light (e.g., dwarf fruit trees, hazelnut, dogwood)
3. **Shrub Layer** — Berry bushes and woody shrubs (e.g., blueberry, currant, gooseberry, elderberry)
4. **Herbaceous Layer** — Perennial herbs, vegetables, and flowers (e.g., comfrey, rhubarb, artichoke, bee balm)
5. **Ground Cover Layer** — Low-growing plants that protect soil (e.g., strawberry, clover, creeping thyme, violets)
6. **Vine / Climber Layer** — Climbing plants that use vertical space (e.g., grape, kiwi, hops, passionflower)
7. **Root / Rhizosphere Layer** — Root crops and tubers (e.g., garlic, horseradish, groundnut, sunchoke)

Some models include an 8th layer:
8. **Mycelial / Fungal Layer** — Mushrooms and soil fungi (e.g., shiitake, oyster mushroom, mycorrhizal networks)

### Types of Food Forests
- **Home Scale Food Forest** — Small, intensive, manageable by one household. Focus on daily yields, kitchen integration, aesthetic appeal. Unique: highest diversity per square foot, most personal connection.
- **Campus Food Forest** — Educational setting, multiple stakeholders, evolves over time. Unique: teaching function, student labor, expansion potential (OSU examples).
- **Campus Orchard** — Subset of campus food forest, tree-focused. Demonstrates how food forests expand incrementally.
- **High Profile Public Food Forest** — High visibility, high traffic areas. Unique: must consider aesthetics, signage, public perception, maintenance standards, liability.
- **Community Food Forest** — Public space, volunteer-maintained, shared harvest (e.g., Malden Court). Unique: collective stewardship, social function, equitable access.
- **Venue Food Forest** — Multi-purpose space combining food production with community events (e.g., West Central Park, Olympia). Unique: concerts, farmers markets, surplus sharing.

### Climate Considerations
- **Temperate climates**: Four seasons, dormancy period, frost considerations, chill hours for fruit trees, seasonal succession planning
- **Tropical climates**: Year-round growth, no dormancy, different layering (taller canopy), rapid succession, high biodiversity potential
- **Desert/Arid climates**: Water is primary design constraint, observation of water movement critical, microclimate creation essential, shade as resource, adapted species
- **Mediterranean climates**: Dry summers, wet winters, fire considerations, drought-adapted species

### Indigenous Forest Management (Key Themes)
- Indigenous peoples worldwide have managed "wild" landscapes for millennia
- Techniques include: controlled burning, horticultural practices, regenerative harvesting, propagation and transportation of species, cultivation and domestication
- The line between "wild" and "cultivated" is a colonial construct — many "wild" landscapes are actually managed food forests
- Moroccan argan forests as example of long-term food forest tradition
- Mayan food forests demonstrate deep integration of food, medicine, and ecology
- PNW indigenous peoples managed forests through fire, camas cultivation, berry management

### Indigenous Forest Management — Deep Dive (Yale E360, Charles M. Peters)
**Core thesis**: Indigenous communities have developed interdependent systems of agriculture and forestry over centuries/millennia that are uniquely suited to their land. Western forestry has largely failed to recognize or learn from this knowledge.

**Key insights:**

**Invisible management**: Much of what outsiders perceive as "pristine" or "virgin" forest has actually been intensively managed by indigenous communities for generations. Peters: you walk through what looks like wilderness and the people say "this is an orchard we created." These managed forests are still forests precisely BECAUSE they are important to local communities.

**Managing at the seedling/sapling stage**: Western foresters manage at the adult tree level. Indigenous systems operate at the seedling and sapling stage — shaping a forest canopy that won't be visible for decades. This is invisible to outsiders but produces lasting structural change. DESIGN PRINCIPLE: think in decades, manage what's regenerating, not just what's mature.

**Polyculture complexity**: The Kenyah Dayak of Borneo manage 150 tree species per hectare. Western foresters can manage 4-5 commercial species at best. This requires intimate knowledge of every species' requirements, regeneration patterns, and relationships. Achieved through trial and error over 1,000+ years of observation. NORTH STAR: Lara's goal is to approach this level of polyculture complexity on her 50 acres over time.

**Enriched fallows (slash-and-burn reframed)**: Indigenous farmers don't abandon cleared areas — they actively enrich fallows with fruit trees, timber species, thatch palms, medicinal plants, and rattan. There is no clear line between agriculture and forestry. Forest succession is carefully controlled rather than arrested. Agricultural plots transition into managed food forests through intentional succession.

**Quantifying demand and supply**: Peters' method — interview the community about what resources they use, then survey the forest to quantify what's available. When you combine demand and supply, you can calculate how much forest is needed to sustain the community. APPLICATION: What does Lara need from her 50 acres? What's already producing? What niches need filling?

**Empowerment through data**: Teaching communities to do their own forest inventories and growth studies empowers them to manage sustainably and advocate for their rights. APPLICATION: The species database and observation log serve this same function — building a data-driven understanding of the property.

**PNW parallel**: Indigenous peoples of the Pacific Northwest (Yakama, Klickitat, and others in the Columbia River Gorge region) managed landscapes through controlled burning for camas prairies, huckleberry fields, and oak savanna maintenance. The "wild" forests of the PNW are shaped by millennia of indigenous management.

**Source**: "Lessons Learned from Centuries of Indigenous Forest Management" — Yale Environment 360, interview with Charles M. Peters by Richard Schiffman, August 2018. Peters is author of *Managing the Wild: Stories of People and Plants and Tropical Forests* (Yale University Press).

### Permaculture Ethics (Foundation)
1. **Earth Care** — Regenerate and protect natural systems
2. **People Care** — Provide for human needs sustainably
3. **Fair Share** — Limit consumption, redistribute surplus

### Permaculture Principles (12 principles from David Holmgren)
1. Observe and interact
2. Catch and store energy
3. Obtain a yield
4. Apply self-regulation and accept feedback
5. Use and value renewable resources and services
6. Produce no waste
7. Design from patterns to details
8. Integrate rather than segregate
9. Use small and slow solutions
10. Use and value diversity
11. Use edges and value the marginal
12. Creatively use and respond to change

### Ecology Fundamentals
- **Relationships** between species and their environment
- **Succession**: how ecosystems develop over time
- **Niche**: the role a species plays in its ecosystem
- **Edge effect**: biodiversity increases at boundaries
- **Guilds**: groups of species that support each other

### Observation Practice
- Foundation of all permaculture design
- Observe before you design — at minimum through all four seasons
- Notice: water flow, sun patterns, wind, existing vegetation, wildlife, soil conditions, microclimates
- Desert climate observation lesson: understanding aridity, water harvesting, working with rather than against climate limitations

## Design North Star: 150-Plant-Species Polyculture
Inspired by the Kenyah Dayak of Borneo who manage 150 tree species per hectare, Lara's long-term goal is to build toward this level of polyculture complexity across her 50 acres. This is a multi-generational aspiration, not a single-season planting plan.

**The path there:**
1. **Inventory what exists** — the native forest already has significant species diversity. Document everything.
2. **Identify gaps by layer and function** — where are the niches that need filling?
3. **Enrich incrementally** — add species season by season, guild by guild, letting each establishment inform the next.
4. **Manage at the seedling stage** — following indigenous practice, shape what regenerates rather than only managing mature plants.
5. **Track everything** — the species database is the tool that makes 150 plant species manageable. Every species gets a row, every observation feeds the system.
6. **Quantify abundance and uses** — Peters methodology: know what you have, what it provides, and how much you can sustainably harvest.
7. **Think in decades** — the canopy you're designing won't be visible for 20-30 years. The understory and shrub layers will show results sooner.

**Progress tracking:** The plant species count in the database (currently **4 of 150 plant species = 2.7%**) is a living metric of progress toward this goal. The total species database also tracks birds, mammals, fungi, and other wildlife as part of the broader ecological inventory.
